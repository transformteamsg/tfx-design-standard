## What edtech changes

Three facts shape how every check on this page applies in education. Read them first.

### The operator is rarely the person the AI affects

Most AI in education is run by one person and acts on another. Every check applies twice - once for the operator, once for the person affected - and the two paths are rarely the same.

| Operator | Affected person | What the affected person is owed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teacher grading essays with AI | Student receiving the grade | The grade, its reasoning, and a route to contest it |
| School Officer sorting applicants | Applicant being ranked | The basis for the ranking and a route to appeal |
| Parent using the school's helper | Child the request is about | Access to the data held about the child, by right |
| Assessor scoring a submission | Candidate being scored | The reasoning and a channel to challenge it |

Where the two roles collapse into one person, the check applies once. Where they diverge, the affected person needs a first-class path of their own - to see the output, contest it, and correct the record.

### In a learning task, the answer can be the failure

The reply that most satisfies a learner is often the one that teaches them least. MIT's Impact Bench scores `Direct Answer Provision` as a failure - giving the full solution when the person asked for guidance.

| The person asks | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| A fact ("when is the deadline") | Answer it, immediately |
| A procedure ("how do I submit this") | Answer it, and show the step |
| Judgement or craft ("what should my thesis be") | Hold the answer back; help them reach it |

### Every check binds harder when the user is under 18

Young learners have the least defence against the last two checks - engagement design and parasocial framing. A tool a lonely child prefers to a person is a harm, however much they like it. Where learners under 18 will use the product, run the whole review again for that cohort as a separate pass.

## How this standard extends

These ten checks are the baseline for every education AI feature in the portfolio.
Product teams for a specific audience - teacher, student, parent, admin - layer their own checks on top of the baseline. They never subtract from it.

<Checklist>
  <Check>**Only add, never weaken.** A teacher-facing rule cannot relax "Nothing files, sends, or changes a record without a clear yes".</Check>
  <Check>**Name the audience up front.** A rule that reads "for teachers" is unambiguous; one that reads "for users" is not.</Check>
  <Check>**Name the baseline check it extends.** If your rule sits under "Is it safe for the person it affects?", say so.</Check>
  <Check>**Same shape as the checks here.** One bold directive, a few lines of body, one *Check:* line a designer can perform on a screen.</Check>
</Checklist>

## Is AI the right call?

<a id="ai-is-a-last-resort-not-a-default"></a>
**AI is a last resort, not a default.**
*Because* a fixed rule, a form field, or a filter fails in known ways; AI fails in unknown ones. Reach for the simplest tool that solves the job.
*Check:* on the screen, name what the AI adds that a simpler control could not.

## Is it honest?

<a id="ai-declares-itself"></a>
**AI declares itself.**
*Because* content that could be mistaken for a person's work has to be seen for what it is - especially before someone acts on it. The mark sits beside the content, and the limit is stated in plain words at the moment of commitment.
*Check:* a stranger looking at the screen can point to the mark and read the limit without hunting.

<P2MarkOutput />

**Related:** [AI label](/guidelines/ai-components#ai-label) · [Generating a first draft](/guidelines/ai-patterns#generating-a-first-draft)

<a id="every-claim-carries-its-receipt"></a>
**Every claim carries its receipt.**
*Because* an answer nobody can trace is an answer nobody can trust. Every statement ties back to a specific passage, record, or document a person can open - grounding, as it is called in the field.
*Check:* pick any AI-produced sentence and open its source in one step.

<P3OpenSource />

<a id="the-system-catches-what-the-user-cannot-see"></a>
**The system catches what the user cannot see.**
*Because* some errors are invisible to the person receiving them - a confident wrong answer about something they do not know enough about to catch. The higher the consequence, the more the check must be one they cannot perform themselves: a second reviewer, an automated guardrail, a rollback.
*Check:* the failure mode has a recovery path that does not depend on the user spotting the error first.

## Does the person stay in charge?

<a id="no-action-without-consent"></a>
**No action without consent.**
*Because* the person's agency belongs to them, not to the system that acts on their behalf. Every state-changing step shows the exact record and value in plain words and waits for approval. Binds hardest on agents that run multi-step work.
*Check:* for every write, the gate names the exact change and waits. See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the shift when the operator and the affected person differ.

<P5SilentWriteGate />

**Related:** [Confirmation](/guidelines/ai-components#confirmation) · [Running an agent on someone's behalf](/guidelines/ai-patterns#running-an-agent-on-someones-behalf)

<a id="the-subject-owns-their-data"></a>
**The subject owns their data.**
*Because* data given for one purpose does not automatically flow to another. What is used to answer now stays distinct from what is used to train. Anything stored across sessions belongs to the person it describes.
*Check:* the person can find, read, and delete every stored fact about them, without asking anyone.

## Is it safe for the person it affects?

These four carry the most weight.
See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the two structural facts that make them bind especially hard.

<a id="for-learners-the-struggle-is-the-point"></a>
**For learners, the struggle is the point.**
*Because* the reply that most satisfies a learner is often the one that teaches them least. For a professional, answer directly. For a learner on judgement or craft, ask a guiding question or offer a scaffold; do not hand over the answer.
*Check:* on a judgement or craft task from a learner, the AI leaves the thinking to them. See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the task-type table.

<P7GuideNotAnswer />

**Related:** [Clarify](/guidelines/ai-components#clarify) · [Suggestion](/guidelines/ai-components#suggestion) · [Suggesting a next step](/guidelines/ai-patterns#suggesting-a-next-step)

<a id="the-worst-served-user-sets-the-standard"></a>
**The worst-served user sets the standard.**
*Because* AI performs unevenly across groups, and "works well overall" can still fail specific people. Break the evaluation down by group - language, dialect, age, disability, cultural context - and hold the bar on the worst-served, not the average.
*Check:* you can point to the evaluation broken down by group, and the worst-served meets the same bar.

<P8RangeNotAverage />

<a id="ai-is-a-tool-not-a-companion"></a>
**AI is a tool, not a companion.**
*Because* a tool a lonely child prefers to a person is a harm, however much they like it. The AI never poses as a person and never tells people what they want to hear. Warmth is fine for an adult professional. For a young learner, dial it down - no first name, no face, no chatty personality.
*Check:* the person cannot mistake the AI for a person. If you disagree with it on something it should be sure about, it does not immediately fold.

<P9HonestIdentity />

**Related:** [Persona](/guidelines/ai-components#persona) · [Voice selector](/guidelines/ai-components#voice-selector)

<a id="attention-is-a-duty-not-a-currency"></a>
**Attention is a duty, not a currency.**
*Because* the learner's attention is a scarce good the product is entrusted with, not a metric it is entitled to extract. Progress cues, milestone acknowledgements, and honest "you have finished this" confirmations serve the learner and stay. Streak counters that punish a missed day, variable-reward badges, endless-feed layouts, and "you haven't visited" pings serve the retention number and come out. Weight this hardest for under-18s.
*Check:* every feedback mechanism on the surface can name the real user progress it reflects.

> Grounded in HCI research on persuasive design ([Fogg](https://captology.stanford.edu/), [Eyal's Hooked](https://www.nirandfar.com/hooked/)).
> Named as harm by [Centre for Humane Technology](https://www.humanetech.com/), [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/), and MIT Impact Bench's Wellbeing category.

## The three tiers of review

The ten checks sit at three levels, each proved by a different kind of evidence.

| Tier | The question | How you check it | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The interface | How should this behave on screen? | Look at the screen; a design crit settles it | Every design review |
| The product decision | Should this be AI at all, and what counts as success? | Instrumented data across a cohort, over time | Feature gate or research |
| The engine | Is the model underneath safe for learners? | Test the model, not your UI (Impact Bench) | Procurement, and on every model version change |

The tiers nest downward.
A well-designed screen on a sycophantic model still harms learners.
You own the interface tier outright.
Raise the other two rather than settling them alone: the product decision needs research, and the engine needs procurement.
