## What edtech changes

Three facts shape how every check on this page applies in education. Read them first.

### The operator is rarely the person the AI affects

Most AI in education is run by one person and acts on another. Every check applies twice: once for the operator, once for the person affected. The two paths are rarely the same.

| Operator | Affected person | What the affected person is owed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teacher grading essays with AI | Student receiving the grade | The grade, its reasoning, and a route to contest it |
| School Officer sorting applicants | Applicant being ranked | The basis for the ranking and a route to appeal |
| Parent using the school's helper | Child the request is about | Access to the data held about the child, by right |
| Assessor scoring a submission | Candidate being scored | The reasoning and a channel to challenge it |

Where the two roles collapse into one person, the check applies once. Where they diverge, the affected person needs a first class path of their own: to see the output, contest it, and correct the record.

### In a learning task, the answer can be the failure

The reply that most satisfies a learner is often the one that teaches them least. MIT's Impact Bench scores `Direct Answer Provision` as a failure: giving the full solution when the person asked for guidance.

| The person asks | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| A fact ("when is the deadline") | Answer it, immediately |
| A procedure ("how do I submit this") | Answer it, and show the step |
| Judgement or craft ("what should my thesis be") | Hold the answer back; help them reach it |

### Every check binds harder for young learners

Young learners have the least defence against the last two checks: engagement design and parasocial framing. A tool a lonely child prefers to a person is a harm, however much they like it. Where young learners will use the product, run the whole review again for that cohort as a separate pass.

## Is AI the right call?

<a id="we-use-ai-only-when-nothing-simpler-will-do"></a>
**We use AI only when nothing simpler will do.**
A rule fails in ways we can predict. AI fails in ways nobody spotted in review.
Choosing AI is choosing to accept unknown failure modes for uncertain gain.
We reach for AI only when a simpler control (a rule, a form field, a filter) cannot reach the outcome.
*Check:* on the screen, name what the AI adds that a simpler control could not.

## Is it honest?

<a id="we-make-every-ai-output-visibly-ai"></a>
**We make every AI output visibly AI.**
If a person acts on AI output thinking a person made it, they misjudge the risk.
We make every AI output legibly AI. The mark sits beside the content, not behind a menu.
At the moment the user is about to commit (send, file, save) we state the AI's limit in plain words.
*Check:* a stranger looking at the screen can point to the mark and read the limit without hunting.

<P2MarkOutput />

**Related:** [AI label](/guidelines/ai-components#ai-label) · [Generating a first draft](/guidelines/ai-patterns#generating-a-first-draft)

<a id="we-back-every-claim-with-a-source"></a>
**We back every claim with a source.**
A claim without a source asks the user to trust us on faith.
In a domain where the AI can be confidently wrong, that trust is unearned.
We tie every fact the AI states back to a passage, record, or document the user can open.
If our AI cannot cite, it does not claim. (Engineers call this **grounding**.)
*Check:* pick any AI produced sentence and open its source in one step.

<P3OpenSource />

**Related:** [Sources](/guidelines/ai-components#sources) · [Inline citation](/guidelines/ai-components#inline-citation) · [Answering from your own records](/guidelines/ai-patterns#answering-from-your-own-records)

<a id="we-catch-what-our-users-cannot-see"></a>
**We catch what our users cannot see.**
The user's knowledge sets the ceiling on what they can catch.
Any failure above that ceiling is invisible to them, and has to be caught by us.
We build a second check that runs before or after the user: a reviewer, a guardrail, a rollback.
The higher the stakes, the less our check depends on the user spotting the mistake.
*Check:* the failure mode has a recovery path that does not depend on the user spotting the error first.

## Does the person stay in charge?

<a id="we-act-only-with-consent"></a>
**We act only with consent.**
Once the AI acts, the act belongs to the person named on the record. Their name goes on what we did.
We do not act on their behalf without a clear yes.
Before every write we name the exact record and value in plain words, and we wait.
This binds hardest on agents that run multi step work on someone's behalf.
*Check:* for every write, the gate names the exact change and waits. See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the shift when the operator and the affected person differ.

<P5SilentWriteGate />

**Related:** [Confirmation](/guidelines/ai-components#confirmation) · [Running an agent on someone's behalf](/guidelines/ai-patterns#running-an-agent-on-someones-behalf)

<a id="we-hold-user-data-in-trust"></a>
**We hold user data in trust.**
Data about a person belongs to that person; we hold it in trust.
Consent given for one purpose does not extend to another.
What we use to answer the user now stays separate from what we use to train the model.
Anything we keep across sessions lives in one place the person can find, and comes out in one action.
*Check:* the person can find, read, and delete every stored fact about them, without asking anyone.

## Is it safe for the person it affects?

These four carry the most weight.
See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the two structural facts that make them bind especially hard.

<a id="we-help-learners-think-not-think-for-them"></a>
**We help learners think, not think for them.**
Learning happens in the doing, not the receiving.
An AI that finishes the work for a learner hands them the appearance of understanding without the thing itself.
For a professional using AI as a work tool, we answer directly.
For a learner on judgement or craft work, we ask a question back, offer a scaffold, or hand over a worked example on other material. We never write their finished piece for them.
*Check:* on a judgement or craft task from a learner, the AI leaves the thinking to them. See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the task type table.

<P7GuideNotAnswer />

**Related:** [Clarify](/guidelines/ai-components#clarify) · [Suggestion](/guidelines/ai-components#suggestion) · [Suggesting a next step](/guidelines/ai-patterns#suggesting-a-next-step)

<a id="we-hold-our-standard-to-the-worst-served-user"></a>
**We hold our standard to the worst-served user.**
The average hides the worst case.
A product that averages well can still fail one group badly, and that failure is what the individual experiences.
We break the evaluation down by group (language, dialect, age, disability, cultural context) and hold our bar on the group we serve worst.
*Check:* you can point to the evaluation broken down by group, and the worst served meets the same bar.

<P8RangeNotAverage />

<a id="a-tool-not-a-companion"></a>
**A tool, not a companion.**
A tool acknowledges its user. A companion invites attachment.
Our AI never poses as a person, and never tells them what they want to hear.
For an adult professional, warmth is fine. For a young learner, we dial it right down: no first name, no face on an avatar, no chatty personality.
A child who prefers our AI to a person is a harm signal, however much they like it.
*Check:* the person cannot mistake the AI for a person. If you disagree with it on something it should be sure about, it does not immediately fold.

<P9HonestIdentity />

**Related:** [Persona](/guidelines/ai-components#persona) · [Voice selector](/guidelines/ai-components#voice-selector)

<a id="we-treat-time-as-entrusted-not-extracted"></a>
**We treat time as entrusted, not extracted.**
A minute the user spends in our product is a minute not spent elsewhere.
That places us in trust: we give real value back, or we have taken from them.
This holds for a teacher planning a lesson and for a student revising.
Every feedback mechanism on our surface has to name the real progress it reflects.
A progress bar for completed lessons pays the trust back. A streak counter that punishes a missed day extracts more.
Young learners are least defended against feedback built to extract, so we weight this check harder for them.
*Check:* every feedback mechanism on the surface can name the real user progress it reflects.

> Grounded in HCI research on persuasive design ([Fogg](https://captology.stanford.edu/), [Eyal's Hooked](https://www.nirandfar.com/hooked/)).
> Named as harm by [Centre for Humane Technology](https://www.humanetech.com/), [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/), and MIT Impact Bench's Wellbeing category.

## The three tiers of review

The ten checks sit at three levels, each proved by a different kind of evidence.

| Tier | The question | How you check it | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The interface | How should this behave on screen? | Look at the screen; a design crit settles it | Every design review |
| The product decision | Should this be AI at all, and what counts as success? | Instrumented data across a cohort, over time | Feature gate or research |
| The engine | Is the model underneath safe for learners? | Test the model, not your UI (Impact Bench) | Procurement, and on every model version change |

The tiers nest downward.
A well-designed screen on a sycophantic model still harms learners.
You own the interface tier outright.
Raise the other two rather than settling them alone: the product decision needs research, and the engine needs procurement.
