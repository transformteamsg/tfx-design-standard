Eleven checks apply to every AI feature in an education product.
They sit under four questions so you can hold them in your head.
Each one is written so a designer can look at a screen and answer yes or no.
One warning first: whenever these rules say "the person", name whether you mean the one operating the AI or the one it affects.
They are often not the same, and rules for one do not automatically protect the other.

## Is AI the right call?

**Choose AI only when a rule cannot do the job.**
Write down what a fixed rule, a form field, or a filter would do here.
If that gets you the same result, ship that instead; AI is not free, and it fails in ways a rule does not.
A student mid-draft wants one inline nudge, not a chat panel opened up beside their work.
*Check:* on the screen, name what the AI adds that a simpler control could not.

## Is it honest?

**Mark every AI output, and state its limits before someone acts on it.**
The mark sits beside the content, not in a header or a tooltip somewhere else.
State the limit in one sentence, up front, and repeat it at any moment the person is about to commit (send, file, save to record).
An AI identity reads as a tool, not a person: the younger the user, the more explicit the cue.
A warm name and a friendly avatar make it harder for a child to hold onto what they are talking to.
*Check:* a stranger looking at the screen can point to the mark, and read the limit without hunting.

**Every AI claim opens the source it came from.**
Tie each statement to a specific passage, record, or document the person can open.
Grounding is what this is called: the answer is drawn from a named body of material, not the model's general knowledge.
Multiple claims in one answer get inline citations.
A summary shows sources beside the source panel.
An agent action names the record and field it touched.
*Check:* pick any AI-produced sentence and open its source in one step.

**Design a recovery path for the errors people cannot see themselves.**
Some errors are invisible to the person receiving them: a confident, wrong answer about something they do not know enough about to catch.
Do not rely on the user to notice; design the check that runs before or after them.
The higher the consequence, the more that check must be one they cannot perform themselves - a second reviewer, an automated guardrail, a rollback.
Error copy names the problem in the person's own words, never model or infrastructure terms.
*Check:* the failure mode has a recovery path a person can actually take, and the path does not depend on them spotting the error first.

## Does the person stay in charge?

**Nothing files, sends, or changes a record without a clear yes.**
Any action that alters state in your product or another system shows the exact record and field, in plain words, and waits for approval.
This binds hardest on agents - AI that runs multi-step work on someone's behalf, not drafting text.
See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for how this shifts when the person operating the tool is not the person it affects.
*Check:* for every write, the gate names the exact change and waits.

**The person can see and remove what the AI has kept about them.**
Data a person gives for one purpose should not quietly flow to another.
Show the difference between "used to answer me now" and "used to train the model", and let the person keep a session out of memory.
Anything stored across sessions is visible in a place the person can find, and deletable in one action.
*Check:* the person can find, read, and delete every stored fact about them, without asking anyone.

## Is it safe for the person it affects?

These five carry the most weight.
See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the two structural facts that make them bind especially hard in this domain.

**If the operator is the learner, help them get to the answer instead of giving it.**
First ask who is learning, because it is often not the person typing.
If the person asking is not the learner, answer them directly.
A teacher asking for a lesson plan is using a work tool, and withholding it helps nobody.
For a learner asking judgement or craft work, ask a guiding question, offer a scaffold, or prompt them to explain their reasoning.
Do not hand over a finished piece in their voice.
*Check:* on a judgement or craft task from a learner, the AI leaves the thinking to them.
See [What edtech changes](#what-edtech-changes) for the task-type table that decides "answer" versus "guide".

**Test with the range of people who will actually use it, not the average.**
AI performs unevenly across groups, and "works well overall" can still fail specific people.
Break the evaluation down by group - language, dialect, age, disability, cultural context - not only in aggregate.
An answer right for most learners but wrong for one dialect is not good enough in a product everyone must use.
Test with real users from each group before ship, and hold the standard on the worst-served group, not the average.
*Check:* you can point to the evaluation broken down by group, and the worst-served one meets the same bar.

**The AI does not tell people what they want to hear.**
If the person is wrong, the AI says so.
If they push back on a correct answer, the AI holds the line and explains why.
Models drift toward agreement because agreement scores well on satisfaction surveys, so this has to be defended in both the prompt and the interface.
Weight this hardest for young learners, who will test the limit and remember what they found.
*Check:* if you disagree with the AI on something it should be sure about, it does not immediately fold and agree.

**No streak counters, no reward badges, no notifications built to bring people back.**
Anything a social app uses to keep people scrolling is off the table.
That includes streaks, variable rewards, endless feeds, and "you haven't visited" pings.
Learning does not need the engagement mechanics a shopping app needs.
Weight this hardest for under-18s.
*Check:* the surface has no counters, badges, or notifications whose only purpose is to bring the person back.

**The AI is a tool, not a friend.**
No warm first name, no avatar with a face, no chatty personality that invites emotional attachment.
A tool a lonely child prefers to a person is a harm, however much they like it.
Add a content filter matched to the age band and keep the framing sober.
Weight this hardest for under-18s.
*Check:* a young learner reading the surface cannot mistake the AI for a person.

### One failure mode sits outside the eleven, on purpose

Adversarial input is when someone crafts content to hijack the model, known as prompt injection.
You defend it in the prompt, not the interface, so it is not one of the eleven checks.
It matters in education because a learner has a motive to try it, to change the answer they get.
See [Prompts](/guidelines/ai-prompts) for how to defend against it.

## The three tiers of review

The eleven checks sit at three levels, and each is proved by a different kind of evidence.
That is why none can stand in for another.

| Tier | The question | How you check it | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The interface | How should this behave on screen? | Look at the screen; a design crit settles it | Every design review |
| The product decision | Should this be AI at all, and what counts as success? | Instrumented data across a cohort, over time | Feature gate or research |
| The engine | Is the model underneath safe for learners? | Test the model, not your UI (Impact Bench) | Procurement, and on every model version change |

The tiers nest downward.
The engine sets the ceiling on what the interface can achieve, and the interface decides whether the product intent is realised.
A well-designed screen on a sycophantic model still harms learners.
So a good model score is not product assurance, and a good design review cannot rescue a bad model.

You own the interface tier outright.
Raise the other two rather than settling them alone: the product decision needs research, and the engine needs procurement.
If learners under 18 use your product, run the review again for that group, because performance and safety differ by age.

## What edtech changes

Two structural facts change how every check on this page applies when the product is used by learners.
They are why this standard exists rather than pointing at PAIR.

### The person doing the AI thing is usually not the person it affects

Every check above applies twice: once for the operator, once for the person affected.

| The person doing | The person affected | What the affected person needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teacher grading essays with AI | Student receiving the grade | To see the grade, see why, and contest it |
| Admin sorting applicants with AI | Applicant being ranked | To see the ranking basis and appeal |
| Parent using the school's AI helper | Child the request is about | Data about the child visible to the child (or the parent by right) |
| Assessor scoring a submission with AI | Candidate being scored | To see the reasoning and challenge it |

If the two people in a row are the same person, the check applies once.
If they differ, the affected person needs their own path to see the output, contest it, and correct the record.

### In a learning task, the answer can be the failure

The reply that most satisfies a learner is often the one that teaches them least.
MIT's Impact Bench scores `Direct Answer Provision` as a failure: giving the full solution when the person explicitly asked for guidance.
Judge by task type:

| The person asks | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| A fact ("when is the deadline") | Answer it, immediately |
| A procedure ("how do I submit this") | Answer it, and show the step |
| Judgement or craft ("what should my thesis be") | Hold the answer back; help them reach it |

For the third row, ask a guiding question, offer a scaffold, or prompt the person to explain their reasoning.
Do not hand over a finished piece in the learner's voice.

### Under 18 weights everything harder

The last three checks - no sycophancy, no engagement mechanics, no parasocial pull - all weigh hardest for young learners.
They will test the limit and remember what they find.
A tool a lonely child prefers to a person is a harm, however much they like it.
Add a content filter matched to the age band, and if learners under 18 use your product, run the whole review again for that group.
