Teachers don't come to our products to read. They come to look up a student, find the right tool, or complete admin tasks. The words along the way exist to move that task forward, not to decorate. Every extra word slows a teacher down, so weigh each one.

UI text is the product's microcopy: words and phrases tied directly to a teacher's actions. Button labels, tooltips, help text, error messages — everything a teacher reads while working.

That job separates UI text from our other writing. Informational content explains how the product works; marketing promotes it; UI text gets the task done with as little reading as possible. So we write in a straightforward, conversational tone, and put clarity and usability ahead of expressive language.

For specific guidance, go deeper with [Grammar & mechanics](/guidelines/grammar-mechanics), [Voice & tone](/guidelines/voice-tone), and [Components & text patterns](/guidelines/text-patterns).

## 1. Clarify the context, then draft

UI text exists to help a user complete a task. Before writing, clarify three things: the user, their goal, and what the product needs the text to do. For an error message, the teacher's goal is to understand what went wrong; the product's goal is to help them recover quickly.

Then write your first draft. Simply write what you want to say to the user. Don't worry; we will polish it later.

Example:

`You have entered an incorrect postal code.`

## 2. Edit to be purposeful

Every word on the screen should serve the teacher's goal and the product's. Edit the first draft to ensure it meets all goals.

When there is more than 1 goal to convey in your writing, order them effectively:

- **Begin with your most important goal.** The first idea is scanned first and is the
  second most memorable goal (primacy effect). This should signal to the users that
  they have found the idea they are seeking.
- **Follow up with any other goals, in order of importance.** Arrange them logically
  and coherently so that users can easily follow along.
- **End with the goal that informs the next step.** The last idea is the most
  memorable (recency effect) and impacts the user's action.

Before:

`You have entered an incorrect postal code.`

Help users recover:

`You have entered an incorrect postal code. Please enter a valid postal code e.g. 310480.`

## 3. Edit to be concise

Teachers come to the interface to complete a task. They scan for the smallest amount of text that helps them understand and act. Write concisely so they can process information quickly and continue their work.

- **Copy should lead with its purpose.** Start with what it does for the teacher and
  when to use it before explaining how. A teacher should understand why something
  exists from the start. Front load essential information, such as the teacher's
  goal, benefit, or action to take ([CNT-5](/standards/catalog/cnt-5)).
- **Use short sentences or phrases.** Keep sentences to 25 words or fewer
  ([CNT-3](/standards/catalog/cnt-3)).
- **Try different arrangements.** Rewrite the same message in a few ways and keep the
  clearest concise option.
- **Remove low value words.** Do this only when clarity and readability stay intact
  ([CNT-6](/standards/catalog/cnt-6)):
  - Empty openers (e.g. `There is`, `There are`, `It is`, `This is`, and `In order to`)
  - Filler words (e.g. `such`, `just`, `that`, `really`, `very`, and `please`)
  - Articles and conjunctions (e.g. `a`, `the`, and `and`)
- **Check for low value words introduced by AI.** Look for buzzwords, filler phrases,
  negative parallelism, copula avoidance, and forced triads
  ([SLP-9](/standards/catalog/slp-9)).

Before:

`You have entered an incorrect postal code. Please enter a valid postal code e.g. 310480.`

Remove words with low informational value:

`Incorrect postal code. Enter valid postal code e.g. 310480.`

## 4. Edit to be conversational

Concision can leave copy sounding robotic. UI text is a conversation: messages and instructions are the product talking to the teacher; buttons and inputs are the teacher replying. Read the copy aloud; if it sounds robotic, rewrite it until it sounds like a person.

- Second person and active voice: `We'll send a reminder`, not `A reminder will be
  sent` ([CNT-3](/standards/catalog/cnt-3), voice and person).
- Replace a buried `to be` verb with a plain action verb: `is a requirement` becomes
  `you must`.
- Watch for actions buried in nouns — they drag a `to be` verb along with them:

| Look for | For example | Use the verb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -tion | recommendation | recommend |
| -ment | assignment | assign |
| -ance | maintenance | maintain, keep |
| -ure | failure | fail |

- Soften sensitive moments. For a disabled account, `We've disabled your account`
  reads warmer than `Your account is disabled` — the product owns the action instead
  of announcing a verdict.

The conversational pass puts the warmth back — and lands on the error shape [CNT-1](/standards/catalog/cnt-1) asks for: what happened, then what to do next.

Example:

`That doesn't look like the right postal code. Please enter a valid postal code e.g. 310480.`

## 5. Edit to be clear

Clarity wins over everything. Clear copy sets expectations and builds trust. After one read, a teacher should know two things: what it means, and what the next step is.

- One idea per sentence; simple present tense where it fits: `Application submitted`,
  not `Application has been submitted`.
- Avoid double negatives: `I want to receive reminders`, not `I do not want to opt
  out of reminders`.
- Unstack nouns — usually only one word is the true noun, and the rest can go back to
  being verbs: `Were you employed continuously?`, not `Were you in continuous
  employment?`.
- Use short and simple words:

| Instead of | Write | Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| attempt | try | required | need |
| inquire | ask | prior to | before |
| initiate | begin | subsequent | next |
| terminate | end | utilise | use |
| obtain | get | purchase | buy |

- Buttons name the action they take: "Save changes", not "Submit".
- A quick exposure test: show someone the screen for five seconds, then ask what it
  means and what to do next. If they can't answer, edit again.

The clear pass spells out what a valid entry looks like, simply.

Example:

`That doesn't look like the right postal code. Please enter at least 6 digits and only numbers.`

The copy is edited. The remaining passes are checks — run them before the text ships.

## 6. Check consistency

One term per thing, everywhere. Switching terms mid-product makes a teacher stop and wonder whether two words mean two things.

- Internal consistency: the same action keeps the same name across the product —
  `Edit`, never a mix of `Amend`, `Change`, and `Modify`.
- External consistency: use the terms teachers already meet across the web instead of
  inventing your own — a search field's placeholder says `Search`, not `Find`.

[CNT-2](/standards/catalog/cnt-2) carries the naming rules; [Naming](/guidelines/naming) is the deep-dive.

## 7. Check readability

- Sentence case everywhere: capitalise the first word and proper nouns only.
- Short sentences, one idea each ([CNT-3](/standards/catalog/cnt-3), sentence ceiling).

[Grammar & mechanics](/guidelines/grammar-mechanics) carries the conventions: capitalisation, numbers, punctuation.

## 8. Check legibility

Legibility is visual, not verbal. Text size, contrast, and alignment belong to the typography and accessibility controls ([TYP-2](/standards/catalog/typ-2) size floors, [A11Y-1](/standards/catalog/a11y-1) contrast), not to this page. If the copy is hard to see, fix it there before rewording anything.

## 9. Check accessibility

Accessible copy works for teachers with motor, vision, or hearing impairments — and reads better for everyone else too.

- Write chronologically, not spatially or visually. Screen readers parse top to
  bottom, so avoid position words (`above`, `below`) and colour references
  (`the blue button`).
- Put important text before the control it informs: above buttons, beside field
  labels. Text placed after a button is announced after it — too late to help the
  decision.
- Link text names its destination: `View the report`, never `click here` or
  `read more` ([CNT-4](/standards/catalog/cnt-4),
  [A11Y-7](/standards/catalog/a11y-7)).
- Name the action, not the device: use `choose`, `select`, or `view` — never
  `click`, `tap`, or `swipe` ([CNT-4](/standards/catalog/cnt-4)).
- Give briefly shown surfaces (toasts, tooltips) enough time to be read before they
  disappear.
- Write informative, unique page titles; in a multi-step flow, include the current
  step ([A11Y-9](/standards/catalog/a11y-9)).

[Components & text patterns](/guidelines/text-patterns) carries the per-surface shapes: errors, empty states, loading, confirmations.

## 10. Check grammar and spelling

Editing for concision may cut articles, and that trade is fine — in microcopy, clarity and scannability outrank strict grammar. Everything that remains must still read cleanly, so proofread the final text. A spelling error breaks trust faster than any style slip. Singapore English spelling (British base): organise, colour, centre.

## 11. Check voice and tone

Our voice is warm, assuring, and respectful — Kind Utility in words. Tone adapts to context: errors stay calm, success stays brief, destructive actions stay sober. [Voice & tone](/guidelines/voice-tone) carries the voice attributes and the tone-by-context table.

## Cut the AI-writing tells

A final pass: [SLP-9](/standards/catalog/slp-9) carries the full list — marketing buzzwords, em-dash chains, filler, and redundant label/helper pairs. When copy sounds impressive but says little, it has already failed.
